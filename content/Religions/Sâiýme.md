Sâiýme is a religion and organization that forms a majority in southeastern Nulâašilu and Sastrānivê, as well as regions of Ancu cultural influence around the Blue Sea's periphery. The religion is derived from a series of traditions related to the pre-Xšaŧan peoples of modern-day Êîlur, some of whom have since migrated to the Nuvê Basin in Iŋatra out of religious persecution.

# Etymology
The word "Sâiýme" translates to "Steward's Devout" in Old Ancu, which was taken directly from the political organization that espoused the faith in the precursor years before the Great Conversion War.

# History

## Fečāna and Ţíazrenu
The story that Fečāna reported as her inspiration during the founding of the Sâiýme is as follows:

In 432 AR, a woman by the name of Fečāna (born in rural Nebur, now part of Iŋatra) was travelling to the Ancu city of Epacre as a slaver delivering slaves, when midway through one of the captives caught her attention. The captive, whose name was Ţíazrenu, was singing an old song of his distant homeland (modern-day E'Đēŧasen) in his own language. After a while, Fečāna finally asked why he was singing it and what it meant, not expecting an answer, as she had assumed he didn't know how to speak Ancu. To her surprise, Ţíazrenu smiled, and explained in perfect Ancu that he was singing a hymn, a hymn of blessing for his captors. Fečāna grew angry, thinking Ţíazrenu was lying and cursing them all for his misfortunes, and ordered him beaten for it. There were no others in the convoy who could speak his native language to confirm or deny his song.

Ţíazrenu continued to sing, and Fečāna ordered continuously harsher and harsher punishments. After 11 days, Fečāna asked him incredulously: "How come you continue to sing, despite all that I've done to discourage you from it? I've had you beaten, starved, and beaten yet more, and still you continue to sing!" Ţíazrenu replied "My resolve to bless and help you turn from your dark path has only grown. I don't care that you've treated me horribly, and I don't care about my life. My life was forfeit years ago. All anyone should want is to follow the 11." Fečāna would still not believe him or his cryptic meaning of the '11', and had him starved further. However, she had misjudged how serious his fragility had already become, and he died in the night, singing to the last. She immediately felt regret for her actions, and when his body was in the process of being buried, she saw an 11-rayed star tattooed on his arm that she hadn't noticed before. His last actions, and his last words spoken to her: they would haunt her, and cause her immense guilt as she started to see more and more of the suffering taking place over various slave convoys. Eventually, she could bear it no more.

She left the slavers, and went to the Ancu capital of Ráemar to search for clues as to his homeland. To various people, she described his appearance, the tattoo, and his song (at which point several verses were engrained into her memory). After a while, a scholar proposed that his home was a tribe known as the Ðāni, a people not too far away in the mountains of Azua. She departed to find his homeland. After flying for a few weeks, asking for further directions and guidance multiple times along the way, she found the tribe. She had brought along a translator, and after explaining the songs and death of Ţíazrenu, tearfully and prepared to die (expecting them to take revenge on her), she waited to see what the tribe leader would do. He stepped up, and proceeded to explain that since Fečāna was clearly telling the truth, and she displayed a true remorse for her deplorable actions, she had already started to follow the sun, in her heart. When she asked what he meant, confused, he replied somewhat cryptically: "The Steward gave us the 11 Tenets. We all must follow the Tenets, else we do not return. Ţíazrenu has. You may yet."

Over several weeks, she started to learn the religion and overall philosophy of the tribe. None in the tribe were really sure as to when they adopted their religion or even what / who prompted it, but all agreed that it was established many generations ago. After writing everything down, she returned to Ráemar and started to preach her newfound faith, singing the songs of the Đāni and spreading the word of Sâî. She established an organization that would come to be known as the Sâiýme, the name of which would eventually become the name of the religion. Whilst travelling Ancurāna, the authorities arrested her for heresy, and executed her in E'Đēŧasen via decapitation. Just like Ţíazrenu, Fečāna died singing the hymn of blessing.

## ## Sâiýme's Early Spread
Fečāna's martyrdom led the Sâiýme organization to evangelize further and further, with public political discontent in conjunction with a natural famine providing the tinder for the spark that was Sâiýme. It seemed to many in the public that their old god had failed them, and that this new religion further from the tyranny of the priests may provide better fortune. Many of the Literamancists, who were tired of being scrutinized by the government to ensure their loyalty, secretly began talks with the Sâiýme for their cooperation in a coup. A sizable percentage of the populace supported the Sâiýme, but the military was largely still supporting the central government. The coup was launched, which involved the imprisonment of the Ancu Emperor and the official designation of Sâiýme as the state religion. A civil war (the Great Conversion War) began when the army regathered in the empire's periphery, which the Sâiýme forces won and cemented their power.

The Sâiýme organization became integrated with the Ancu state, and the name of the organization from then on became the name of the religion, with the adherents thenceforth known as the Iýmah.

# Beliefs
There are 3 Planes: The Plane of Hūra (The Plane of Existence), the Plane of Sâî (The Plane of Liminality), and the Plane of Âdra (The Plane of Souls).

Adherents of Sâiýme, the Iýmah, believe that the prime figure in the religion (Sâî) did not necessarily create the physical universe (leaving that to empirical science) but rather is a steward, or a caretaker, of the physical universe from another plane of reality: ensuring that everything continues to run smoothly, and one who guides the souls of the deceased to Âdra—their equivalent of the afterlife, and also pre-birth. Âdra is also a deity of sorts, despite Sâî being more relevant in the religion.

Iýmahs believe that all sentience comes from Âdra, and that light is what represents Âdra on the Universe's plane (Hūra). Âdra is the dwelling of souls, and for a soul to mature it must undergo a rite of passage upon the Plane of Hūra. If a soul does not follow the 11 tenets, then it cannot complete the rite of passage, and is stuck in a liminal state within the Plane of Sâî after its death on the Plane of Hūra, (hence why the Plane of Sâî is known as the Plane of Liminality). Eventually, the soul will fade away to non-existence, Sâî unable to save it or correct it. If the soul is successful though, in following the 11 tenets, then the rite of passage is complete and liminality is not a threat. The soul returns to Âdra - The Plane of Souls - now whole, with the experience of life.

The Iýmah believe that Sâî set out 11 tenets for the Reithi to follow, in order to keep peace with the world, each other, others, and themselves. 

>Empiricism, rationality, honesty, justice, dutifulness, patience, respect, tolerance, kindness, empathy, and faith. 

While it may seem that the tenets of empiricism and faith contradict, it is because Sâî is above the universe that can be observed and scientifically ascertained; he follows different laws. Sâî encourages empiricism, and questioning all knowledge regarding the universe and even religious interpretation. For many, the existence of Literamancy, the Nexus, and the Catenas are proof enough for the Iýmah that not all things follow the same laws as others, and are proof of Sâî's direct intervention into their world. Empiricism describes what can be described, like the Physical Universe (Existence) and Literamancy, whilst faith describes what cannot be, like the link between different planes. Hence, faith is still a tenet.

There are other moral codes within Sâiýme, but all can be classified into the main 11.

Iýmah believe that all sentience comes from Âdra, and that light is what represents Âdra on Existence's plane. Hence, light is holy and is considered the giver of life, and concentrations of it (like stars) are holy manifestations—although not holy in their own right, for the light sources are mere vessels of what is holy. Gravity is another manifestation, they believe, representing loyalty and kindness. Âdra is one of three planes. The Plane of Hūra (Existence), the Plane of Sâî, and the Plane of Âdra. The Universe is composed of all 3 planes.

Iýmah are, however, not above using violence to enforce their means and to achieve their goals, especially in historical contexts when their fledgling faith was under constant threat. Iýmah militants claim that this does not breach the 11 tenets, because if the violence is justified then it falls under "justice" and supersedes "tolerance". As with many religions, there is a moral grey area that people exploit.

The holy sites of Sâiýme include Ráemar (where the Sâiýme organization was founded, and the site of the Ancu Catena), Salūah (which is believed to be the rough location of the Đani tribe) E'Đēŧasen (where Fečāna was martyred), and the road from Ēpacre to Xexuêas (along which, at an unknown location, Ţíazrenu died).

# Cultural Influence
There is no Sâiýme holy book or script, rather a collection of different works that are collectively considered reference material. There is the Tome of Sâî, but it is not truly holy for Sâî is not god.

Worship is a relatively individualistic experience, for to follow the 11 tenets one must practice the virtues yourself. The Iýmah worship the Plane of Âdra, which is simultaneously viewed as a location and a deity of sorts, yet not formally considered god. A common prayer involves praying that Âdra will view them as worthy again to complete Liminality, and once again join Âdra as a completed soul. 

Sâî is not worshipped in Sâiýme, as he is but the steward, and can be prayed to. Sâî is viewed as a fatherly figure who sometimes intervenes in life to help people achieve rejoining Âdra.

There are Sâiýme temples and monasteries, but they are not organized by any overarching theological authority. Sâiýme enforces its organization by using morality and the Tome of Sâî instead. One is expected to demonstrate the 11 tenets, and to observe a series of periods of exaltation demonstrating their adherence. These periods are specified in the Tome of Sâî, and are relatively uniform across cultures and regions.

Monasteries and temples tend to look less like buildings, and rather like a series of pavilion-like structures, linked by roofed walkways. There are few large gathering spaces - instead, the clergy will go between various groups of people and meet with them more personally under each pavilion. Larger convocations can be arranged in the garden spaces interspersed through the temple complexes.

## Possible Felaric Influences
While not a Felaric religion in its own right (not guided by nor inspired by High Felari) the Plane of Liminality has a resemblance to the Dark Field. Since the 'Plane of Liminality' is not a worshipped aspect of the religion, the religion is deemed Antefelaric. 

Furthermore, the religion was founded before the large-scale interference in Reithi affairs by the Felari. Possible contact with Low Felari and travelling tales may have influenced the religion's infancy, but the extent of it is debated.

# Holidays

## Ēsene
Ēsene ("Teaching") is held to be the day when Fečāna arrived in the Ðāni tribe to learn about Ţíazrenu and his past. Customarily held on 3 Ítusrān, work is forbidden and it is a day of reflection, learning, and communal gathering. 

The decentralized nature of worship means people often celebrate it in different ways, but a common practice is to take a long walk from their home to the local temple or library, emulating the journey of Fečāna to the Đāni. In some cultures Ēsene is extended to include the days before and after it, because of uncertain datekeeping. People who do this may extend their walks or activities as a way to visit family and friends in neighbouring cities and villages.

Throughout the hours of the day, emulating the 11 Tenets is considered paramount, transcending the (generally) decentralized practices.

## Henāŧaŋ
Henāŧaŋ ("Shroud of Death") is the remembrance of Fečāna's death in E'Đēŧasen, on the 11 days about 8 Ulberān. While not the true date of her death (having occurred 4 days before), the symbolism of the death happening on one of the darker days of the year in Ráemar (the double new moons and low sun) supplanted historical fact after the centuries. 

Regardless of the exact date, the 11 days around Henāŧaŋ are held to be the holiest of Sâiýme. There is no one way to best celebrate, only social custom in conjunction with following the Tenets.

The first and last days are held to be feasts by most, but more conservative sects eschew this in favour of fasting for the 11 days. Many people paint an 11-rayed star on their arm or forehead, and mark off the tips of the stars with ink for each of the days, surrounding the star with writing personal to that person and the relevant Tenet.
